# The Shotgun and Dogtrot House

*Two Southern house forms, two very different ways of organizing a straight passage from front to back.*

## Introduction

Some houses announce their intelligence through imposing façades. Others reveal it in a plan so simple that its logic is easy to overlook. The shotgun house and the dogtrot belong to the second group.

Both make unusually direct connections from front to back, but they do so in almost opposite ways. In a traditional shotgun, a series of rooms unfolds along a narrow interior axis, with little or no hallway. In a dogtrot, two enclosed sections face one another across a roofed, open-air passage. One compresses domestic life into a line; the other gives the line over to the breeze.

The two forms share Southern associations, modest building traditions, and an economy of space. They should not, however, be treated as interchangeable—or as products of a single historical lineage. Their differences reveal how vernacular builders responded to land, climate, household routines, construction methods, and cultural exchange.

## At a glance

**The essential distinction:** A **shotgun** places rooms **in line**. A **dogtrot** places rooms **on either side of an open-air passage**.

| | Shotgun house | Dogtrot house |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic plan** | One room wide; rooms arranged one behind another | Two enclosed rooms or wings separated by a central passage |
| **The straight-through route** | Typically passes *through* interior rooms | Passes *between* rooms, under a continuous roof |
| **Typical original scale** | Narrow, usually one story; length varies | Typically one story, often with two original “pens” |
| **Characteristic setting** | Southern urban neighborhoods and rural communities | Rural South and Texas frontier settlements |
| **Exterior clues** | Slim street frontage, front-facing door and porch, long side elevation | Two enclosed halves, visible central breezeway, broad common roof |
| **Key tradeoff** | Excellent use of a narrow lot; limited privacy | Shade and outdoor circulation; exposed central passage |
| **Common later alterations** | Side hall, rear ell, enlarged kitchen, added bathroom | Breezeway enclosed, rear rooms added, second story or loft |

**Remember it:** *Shotgun: walk through the rooms. Dogtrot: walk between the rooms.* This is a memorable rule of thumb, not a guarantee that every surviving house retains its original plan.

## Why group these houses together?

The connection is not simply that both can offer a line of sight from front to back. Many unrelated houses can do that. What makes this pairing worthwhile is that **the front-to-back route actually organizes the entire plan**.

In a shotgun, the path is essentially the sequence of rooms. In a dogtrot, the path is a separate, roofed outdoor space, with rooms opening onto it from either side. Put the diagrams side by side and the difference becomes immediate. One plan prioritizes the efficiency of narrow frontage; the other makes the in-between space integral to living in a hot climate.

That comparison also teaches a larger architectural principle: a house type is defined less by its decoration than by how its spaces are arranged and used. Either form may carry varied porch details, siding, roof treatments, and ornamental styles without losing its underlying identity.

## The shotgun: a house arranged in a line

Picture a small Southern cottage whose front door opens directly into its first room. Beyond that is another room, then another, each connected by aligned openings. Traditional single shotguns are commonly one room wide and three to five rooms deep, although actual plans vary. Because there is little or no dedicated hallway, movement to the back of the house often means passing through spaces used for sitting, eating, or sleeping.

The footprint is remarkably economical. On a narrow parcel, almost the entire width of the lot can be assigned to useful rooms instead of a passage beside them. The familiar image is a long, one-story timber-frame building with a small front porch, close neighboring houses, and a gabled or hipped roof. In New Orleans, houses often sit near the sidewalk, making the porch a direct point of contact between household and street.

These are not necessarily primitive or architecturally plain dwellings. Elaborate brackets, turned columns, decorative doors, and carefully detailed cornices can transform a modest plan into an exceptionally expressive façade. The house form remained consistent even when its external language changed.

### Single, double, and camelback shotguns

A **single shotgun** is one narrow sequence of rooms. A **double shotgun** places two such sequences side by side, frequently as separate dwellings, behind a broader façade. A **camelback shotgun** adds a partial upper story, often toward the back, creating additional sleeping space without changing the building's essential narrow character.

Those variations matter because “shotgun” does not describe only one size, one occupancy arrangement, or one decorative style. It names a recognizable spatial family.

## The dogtrot: a house organized around air

Imagine two small cabins built side by side, but not touching. Extend a single roof over both and over the gap between them. That roofed gap is the **dogtrot**, also called a *dog-run* or *open passage*. Traditionally, it is open at the front and back, allowing movement and air through the center of the building.

Many early examples were built from logs and consisted of two single-room units, or *pens*. A room in one pen might be used for cooking or other household work, while the other provided sleeping or living space; exact uses depended on the household. Exterior end chimneys, a full-width front porch, and a simple side-gabled roof were common, though not universal. Later houses could be built with timber framing and weatherboard instead of logs.

The breezeway served several purposes. It offered shade, a sheltered place to sit or work, a practical point of circulation, and a way to let prevailing breezes pass through the house. Crucially, it was not simply an oversized hallway: **it was an outdoor room at the center of domestic life**.

Some dogtrots developed in stages. A family could begin with a single cabin, construct a second nearby, and join the two beneath a continuous roof. Other buildings were planned in the two-part arrangement from the outset. As needs changed, owners frequently enclosed the passage—sometimes making the original type harder to recognize.

## Origins and historical development

### The shotgun's complicated history

Shotgun houses are especially associated with **New Orleans**, where early local examples appeared by the 1830s and the type became widespread during the nineteenth century. Their deeper ancestry, however, is a subject of serious architectural-historical debate.

One influential line of scholarship, associated with folklorist **John Michael Vlach**, traces important precedents through Haiti to West African building traditions. Scholars have also studied New Orleans's Creole cottages, French colonial influences, migration, and the economics of narrow urban lots. More recent interpretations emphasize cultural exchange rather than a single inventor or a simple one-way transmission of a design.

These discussions matter because describing shotgun houses merely as cheap responses to narrow lots can erase the contributions of African and Afro-Caribbean builders and communities. Conversely, no single proposed chain of origins should be presented as universally settled. Historian **Jay D. Edwards** describes the shotgun's history as a contested subject shaped by both evidence and the politics of preservation.

The popular explanation of the name—that a shot fired through the front door could pass straight out the back—is memorable but should be treated as folklore rather than a proven etymology. Another frequently repeated story, that New Orleans property taxes were based on street frontage and therefore caused shotgun houses, is not supported by the Preservation Resource Center of New Orleans.

The type spread beyond Louisiana, including to Louisville and other Southern communities. Its history encompasses urban neighborhoods, rural houses, worker housing, and multiple social and economic contexts.

### The dogtrot and the rural South

The dogtrot is closely associated with nineteenth-century vernacular housing across the **rural South**, including Texas. Builders commonly used readily available timber, straightforward joinery, and incremental construction. A central breezeway suited hot seasons before mechanical cooling, while the separate rooms made it possible to organize household functions around an outdoor heart.

The **Sam E. Johnson Sr. Cabin**, preserved at Lyndon B. Johnson National Historical Park in Texas, is a particularly clear example. Its original west section dates to 1856; the east section was added around 1868. The National Park Service describes the open passage as welcome relief during the summer heat. [National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/places/000/sam-e-johnson-sr-cabin.htm)

As households grew, dogtrots often changed considerably. A central opening could be enclosed, rear sheds added, or a loft introduced. These changes testify to continued use rather than failure of the original type—but they can obscure the building's once-open center.

## Architectural details, materials, and variations

A shotgun may have wood weatherboards, a raised foundation, paired windows, porch brackets, shutters, or decorative Victorian-era millwork. Its ornament can be exuberant even when its plan is disarmingly plain. New Orleans examples include refined façades that give the building a public presence far greater than its narrow width might suggest.

Dogtrots tend to express their structure more directly: log walls, broad side-gabled roofs, full-width porches, exterior chimneys, and a central opening visible from the front elevation. Timber-framed and more finished examples can be notably elegant, especially when the roofline and breezeway are given generous proportions.

Neither type has a mandatory color palette. Historic paint schemes depend on time, region, available materials, and individual alteration. Color should not be used as the principal test of whether a house belongs to either family.

**A useful distinction:** A breezeway added to a modern house does not automatically make it an authentic historic dogtrot; similarly, a long, narrow building is not necessarily a shotgun unless its room sequence and circulation share the defining linear arrangement.

## How the plans shape everyday life

The shotgun's efficiency comes with a privacy challenge. Passing from living room to bedroom to kitchen may mean walking through spaces occupied by other people. Early plans reflect different assumptions about privacy and household life than many contemporary residents hold. A renovation often has to reconcile that sequence with bathrooms, storage, modern kitchens, and rooms that can close off from one another.

The dogtrot has a different challenge: its signature passage is exposed to outdoor temperature, humidity, insects, and weather. In the climates where it developed, however, that openness was a positive feature, especially in summer. The separate halves can also create a remarkably pleasant sense of retreat—distinct zones linked by a shaded common space.

Neither design should be romanticized as universally comfortable without modification. Their value lies in understanding what the original arrangement accomplished and adapting its best qualities thoughtfully to present-day needs.

## Preservation and contemporary adaptation

### Renovating a shotgun

The first task is to identify which changes are central to the original form. Where possible, retain the narrow proportions, front porch, historic windows, and readable sequence of spaces. Carefully located bathrooms, built-in storage, and a sympathetic rear addition can make the plan more livable without turning the house into an unrecognizable shell.

Introducing a side corridor may resolve circulation, but it can also consume a substantial share of the narrow floor plate. Sometimes selective door reconfiguration, discreet openings, or reorganized room functions offer better results. Any structural wall changes, historic-district review, or moisture and foundation issues should be assessed by qualified professionals before work begins.

### Renovating a dogtrot

The most consequential decision is whether to **retain the central breezeway as genuinely open-air space**. Enclosing it may add conditioned square footage, but it removes a defining architectural and climatic feature. Alternatives include screened passages, carefully detailed retractable closures, or a new conditioned connector located where it does not erase the historic open axis.

Modern architects also borrow the dogtrot principle for new houses: two relatively independent volumes linked by an open covered passage. Such interpretations can create welcoming outdoor rooms while distinguishing new construction from documented historic examples.

In both forms, the goal is not to freeze a dwelling in time. It is to understand the plan well enough that improvements feel like a continuation of its logic rather than an argument against it.

## How to recognize each form

**Look for the route first, not the trim.** A shotgun usually presents a slim street-facing façade; when its plan is accessible, the sequence of rooms progresses toward the back without a conventional parallel hallway. A dogtrot usually shows a central roofed opening between two enclosed halves, with doors facing into that passage. Both may have been significantly altered, so exterior appearance alone will not always establish the original type.

Seen together, they offer a valuable lesson in architecture's relationship to everyday life. The shotgun turns the necessity of moving through a small house into its organizing principle. The dogtrot turns the necessity of air and shade into an entire room without walls. Their shared straight-through logic is fascinating precisely because it produces two so different ways of living.
