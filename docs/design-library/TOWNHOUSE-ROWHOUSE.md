**Editorial positioning:** A house-type article about attached urban living, not a single architectural style.

A row of houses is more than an efficient way to occupy a city block. At its best, it creates a rhythm of doors, windows, stoops, and small architectural differences that makes a street feel like a place. The individual houses may be modest or grand, centuries old or newly built. Together, they form one of urban residential architecture’s most enduring compositions.

In American usage, *townhouse* and *rowhouse* often describe closely related attached dwellings. The distinction is partly regional and conventional rather than absolute. To understand either, begin not with the façade, but with the relationship between the home, its neighbors, its narrow parcel, and the public street.

## At a glance

| Essential | The townhouse and rowhouse |
|---|---|
| **House form** | Typically a tall, relatively narrow home sharing one or both side walls with adjoining dwellings |
| **Typical setting** | Urban blocks, historic neighborhoods, walkable mixed-use districts, and newer attached-home developments |
| **Typical levels** | Often two to four stories, though heights vary widely |
| **Defining features** | Repeated street fronts, individual entrances, compact footprints, shared walls, and front-to-back room organization |
| **Outdoor space** | Stoops, small front gardens, rear yards, courtyards, roof terraces, or balconies |
| **Architectural styles** | Federal, Greek Revival, Italianate, Victorian, Colonial Revival, contemporary, and many others |
| **Materials** | Brick, stone, brownstone sandstone, stucco, wood siding, metal, and modern cladding systems |
| **Key advantage** | A full-sized private home on a comparatively small urban parcel |
| **Common challenge** | Bringing daylight and good circulation into a deep, narrow plan |

**The distinction that matters:** *Rowhouse* describes a house’s place in an attached row; *townhouse* is a broader, flexible term often used for multi-story urban homes, attached historic houses, and newly developed residences. Neither term tells you the architectural style, and neither guarantees a particular ownership arrangement.

## What makes a townhouse or rowhouse?

Most rowhouses stand shoulder to shoulder, sharing walls along their long sides and turning narrow faces toward the street. Each commonly has its own front door and internal living spaces stacked vertically. The repeating pattern can form an uninterrupted terrace, a short cluster of attached homes, or an entire block.

The language is less exact than the construction. In Philadelphia and Baltimore, *rowhouse* is deeply embedded in local identity. In New York, *townhouse* may suggest an elegant multistory home, whether or not it belongs to a continuous row. In contemporary real estate, *townhome* can describe anything from a traditional attached street-facing dwelling to a residence within a planned development. Those are usage patterns, not strict universal architectural definitions.

An end unit may have windows on three sides; an interior unit often has exterior openings primarily at the front and rear. That difference has consequences for daylight, ventilation, furniture placement, and potential extensions. Shared walls also demand attention to fire separation, sound control, and maintenance responsibilities.

A **brownstone**, despite common shorthand, is not a separate house form. The term properly refers to a type of sandstone used on many historic New York façades and stoops; its color varies. A brick rowhouse may have a brownstone stoop; a townhouse need not contain brownstone at all.

## A brief history of living in a row

Attached urban housing long predates the American city. In the growing cities of the United States, rows allowed builders to divide valuable land into manageable parcels while giving households individual street entrances. The form suited commercial streets, port neighborhoods, industrial districts, and prestigious residential addresses alike.

Philadelphia offers a particularly revealing example. Its small early *Trinity* houses compressed daily life into compact vertically stacked rooms, while larger Georgian, Federal, and Victorian houses demonstrated how much variety a narrow site could accommodate. The city’s own rowhouse preservation literature describes a remarkable continuum—from working-class homes to imposing residences around Rittenhouse Square.

New York developed an equally recognizable streetscape, with Federal and Greek Revival rows followed by Italianate and later Victorian-era houses. Elevated stoops, carefully composed window openings, stone lintels, decorative cornices, and occasional bay windows could give a long block visual coherence without making every home identical.

The twentieth century expanded the vocabulary. Some suburbs adopted attached housing for its efficient use of land; later New Urbanist developments revived the idea that a row of front doors, small blocks, and pedestrian-scaled streets could support a more connected neighborhood. Contemporary townhomes now appear in both established cities and newly developed metropolitan districts.

## How the plan works

A narrow lot often encourages **vertical zoning**: public rooms below, sleeping rooms above, and storage or service spaces at the basement or rear. The arrangement varies by region and age. A historic house might have a parlor level raised above the street, with a service level below; a new townhouse may put a garage or entry on the ground floor and living areas on the second.

Stair location matters enormously. A straight run against a party wall can conserve frontage; a centrally placed stair can organize rooms on either side; a rear stair may preserve a long entertaining room. The best plan is not necessarily the most open. In a slender house, a sequence of rooms can create a welcome sense of depth and discovery.

**Daylight is the central design puzzle.** Interior units commonly rely on front and rear windows, so long stretches of opaque partitions can leave the center dim. Designers can improve perceived spaciousness with carefully aligned doorways, borrowed light through interior glazing, lighter finishes, a stairwell that carries daylight between levels, and—when feasible and approved—skylights or carefully considered rear additions.

Rear courts and gardens, though small, can matter disproportionately. They provide visual relief, outdoor dining space, and an opportunity to connect the kitchen or living room directly to planting and natural light. Roof decks offer another option where structure, access, code, neighbors, and historic restrictions permit.

## The architecture of the streetscape

An isolated townhouse can be handsome. A well-composed *row* can be extraordinary because its architecture works at two scales at once: the detail of the individual doorway, and the rhythm of the entire block.

Look for repeated cornice lines, aligned windows, consistent setbacks, recurring stoop heights, and a limited but expressive palette of materials. Within that discipline, changes in entry color, masonry pattern, window trim, ironwork, or roof profile can create individual identity.

The streetscape also reveals a subtle social purpose. A low stoop or shallow front garden mediates between private interior and public sidewalk. A higher stoop adds privacy and ceremony. A front porch encourages lingering; an attached garage facing the sidewalk can interrupt that human-scale rhythm. None of these outcomes is automatic, but the relationship between entrance and street is fundamental to the type.

## Five regional and contemporary expressions

### New York: the brownstone townhouse

The familiar New York brownstone presents a strong urban façade, frequently with a raised stoop, tall vertically proportioned windows, ornate door surround, and substantial cornice. Its association with Brooklyn and Manhattan is deserved, but the rowhouse form is more varied than the word *brownstone* suggests. Brownstone is a finish material and a local architectural identity, not a universal requirement.

### Philadelphia: the working and middle-class rowhouse

Philadelphia’s rows include restrained brick fronts, narrow two- and three-story examples, decorative cornices, and houses with elegant bay windows. A cohesive brick streetscape can look almost architectural in the aggregate: individual homes become a single, carefully modulated wall of the city. The city’s rowhouse tradition is unusually broad in size, age, and social history.

### Kentlands, Maryland: the neotraditional townhome

Kentlands in Gaithersburg offers a different lesson. Begun in the late 1980s, it was planned by Andrés Duany and Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk as an influential New Urbanist community, with connected streets, varied housing types, and shops and amenities closer to residences. Its townhomes borrow traditional architectural languages but belong to a consciously planned late-twentieth-century neighborhood. The goal is a streetscape that feels varied and walkable rather than a single repeated building design.

### Contemporary cities: the modern townhome

Modern attached housing may use flat roofs, larger windows, recessed balconies, brick or fiber-cement panels, and minimalist entrances. Often three or four levels high, these homes can incorporate compact garages, roof terraces, and flexible workspaces. Their success depends less on a fashionable façade than on convincing proportions, durable construction, daylight, and a respectful relationship with the street.

### Pacific Northwest: the wood-clad urban row

In the Northwest, local building traditions and the availability of timber have produced attached homes with wood siding, projecting bays, turned trim, and pitched roofs. These may share the underlying urban logic of their eastern counterparts while looking completely different. The lesson is worth emphasizing: material and stylistic vocabulary can change dramatically without changing the fundamental house form.

## Materials and colors

Historic masonry rows can be visually unified through brick color, mortar joints, stone bases, and restrained window trim. New York examples may feature brownstone, limestone, or brick. Philadelphia streets show the expressive potential of brickwork alongside painted doors and cornices. Northwest examples often use painted wood siding, while contemporary designs may combine masonry with metal, glass, and manufactured cladding.

Color works best when considered at both house and block scale. A deeply colored front door can create identity within a consistent brick terrace. A varied row of townhomes can use neighboring colors harmoniously if trim, openings, and massing retain a coherent rhythm. For historic buildings, research surviving finishes and preservation guidance before assuming a modern palette is historically authentic.

There is no single *townhouse color scheme*. The available materials, period, region, and relationship to the surrounding block should guide the decision.

## Advantages and tradeoffs

Townhouses can offer multiple bedrooms, distinctly zoned living areas, private entrances, and a strong neighborhood setting without requiring the land of a detached residence. Shared walls also reduce exposed exterior wall area and can help thermal performance, although actual energy use depends on the building envelope and systems.

The tradeoffs are real. Stairs can be inconvenient; deep plans can have dark centers; parking may be limited or consume valuable frontage. Noise transmission between units depends on shared-wall assemblies, not simply on whether the buildings look substantial. Historic buildings can present structural or moisture issues, and additions are often constrained by property lines, adjacent foundations, utilities, or preservation rules.

Crucially, **house type does not establish ownership**. An attached dwelling can be fee-simple, condominium, or rental housing. Property lines, easements, association documents, and maintenance obligations must be verified rather than inferred from appearances.

## Renovating a townhouse thoughtfully

The most effective renovations respect both the house and its neighbors. Improving a kitchen at the rear may be straightforward; opening a load-bearing party wall is not. Stair modification, roof decks, new windows, and rear extensions can trigger structural review, fire-separation requirements, waterproofing challenges, and permits.

For older masonry rows, investigate moisture pathways, flashing, parapets, aging mortar, stoops, and roof drainage before cosmetic work. Mortar and masonry repairs should be compatible with the existing materials. At the same time, don't mistake historical character for an obligation to preserve every inconvenient interior partition. A well-considered renovation can improve circulation and daylight while retaining the entrance sequence, stair craftsmanship, window proportions, and other meaningful features.

Newer townhomes bring different opportunities: better acoustic separation where accessible, more functional storage, stronger connections to balconies and roof terraces, and improved lighting in deep plans. Good design often involves making the limited width feel intentional rather than disguising it.

**Studio Dee perspective:** In a narrow house, the most valuable intervention may be the one that gives daylight a longer path and makes transitions between rooms more graceful—not necessarily the one that removes the most walls.

## Townhouses and rowhouses in Portland

Portland does not have the same vast continuous nineteenth-century masonry-rowhouse fabric as Philadelphia or New York. Its historical material story is different: a smaller number of documented attached rows sits within a broader wood-frame residential fabric, alongside a growing population of contemporary infill townhomes.

One especially notable exception is the **Campbell Townhouses** in Northwest Portland, constructed in **1893**. The six attached brick units at NW Irving Street and NW 17th Avenue feature Queen Anne detailing and are recorded in Oregon’s Historic Sites Database. Their relative rarity illustrates both the shared lineage and the regional differences of townhouse building.

For local renovation projects, begin with the actual building's historic status, physical condition, and property arrangement. The City of Portland's Historic Resource Inventory can help identify documented resources; any prospective work still requires separate confirmation of applicable zoning, building permits, and preservation rules.
